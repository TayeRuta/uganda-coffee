How Uganda compares with the world's largest coffee producers and with Africa's, on the measures USDA and the International Coffee Organization use: scale, growth, yield, price, stability and home consumption. Peers are chosen by rule, comparisons are made like for like, and where the data is weak or the sources disagree, the gap is shown rather than smoothed over.

Key takeaways

  1. Uganda is the world's 6th-largest producer and exporter, and Africa's largest exporter, with about 4.6% of world exports. High
  2. It is the fastest-growing of the ten largest producers, while most West and Central African robusta producers have shrunk. High
  3. Its biggest gap is yield: perhaps a fifth of Vietnam's per hectare. But the figure rests on imputed area, so the size of the gap is uncertain. Low
  4. It is paid about 85% of the world reference for its mix: more than Vietnam and the other African robusta exporters, less than India and Indonesia. Medium
  5. It drinks almost none of its own coffee (about 4%), unlike Ethiopia, Brazil or Indonesia, and its output is moderately stable. High
  6. Its export figures differ by source by 22–25% in the last two years. Cross-country comparisons here use USDA so every country is measured alike. High

The biggest gap, and the least certain number

Official area figuresEstimated or imputed area
Why it's uncertainFAOSTAT's harvested area for Uganda is imputed in every year from 2020 to 2024, so its yield is production divided by an estimated area. Much of Uganda's coffee is grown by smallholders among bananas and food crops, which makes "area" itself hard to define. The direction of the gap is clear (Vietnam's intensive, irrigated, fertilised farms are in a different league), but not its size. A measured yield survey is the single most valuable missing number in Uganda's coffee data.

Three sources, three numbers

Why it mattersUSDA matched the Coffee Department almost exactly until 2021, then diverged. Possible reasons include coffee from neighbouring countries (eastern DR Congo, South Sudan) exported through Uganda, the treatment of stocks, and USDA building its estimates from production rather than customs records. On USDA figures, Uganda's growth since 2021 is much smaller than national figures show. This benchmark uses USDA for every country so all are measured alike.
07 · What it means

Where Uganda can close the gaps

Productivity

Yield is the big lever

  • Even closing part of the yield gap with Vietnam would add more coffee than all of Uganda's recent planting.
  • India and Indonesia show robusta systems with modest yields can still earn more per kg.
  • First, measure it: Uganda's yield is the least reliable number in the comparison.
Price

Grade and process

  • Uganda already out-earns Vietnam and its African robusta peers per kg.
  • India and Indonesia earn more for their mix, partly through washed and specialty grades.
  • Uganda's arabica discount (earlier report) is the clearest place to gain.
Resilience

Markets at home and data

  • A domestic market of 4% leaves Uganda fully exposed to world prices.
  • Ethiopia and Brazil show what a home market can absorb.
  • Reconciling export statistics with USDA and FAOSTAT would make Uganda's growth story credible to buyers and lenders.

08 · Methods and limits

How this was done

Method

  • Peers by rule: at least 1% of world production, or an African producer above 100,000 bags (USDA, 2021–2025)
  • Groups by USDA robusta share: 70% or more robusta-led, 30% or less arabica-led
  • Growth from five-year averages at each end; volatility as the standard deviation of yearly log changes, 2000–2025
  • Price realisation: export unit value ÷ (robusta share × world robusta price + arabica share × world arabica price), 2020–2024

Limits

  • FAOSTAT area and yield are estimated or imputed for many African producers, Uganda included
  • Export unit values average across grades, destinations and coffee forms
  • USDA figures are estimates built from production, not customs records
  • The robusta/arabica split is USDA's; national figures differ slightly

Sources. USDA Foreign Agricultural Service, coffee production, supply and distribution. FAOSTAT green coffee production and trade. IMF world coffee prices, via FRED. Coffee Department, Ministry of Agriculture, Animal Industry and Fisheries.
