How much of the coffee boom reached Uganda's farmers?

Uganda now exports more than twice as much coffee as it did a decade ago, and in 2024–25 world prices more than tripled. Six decades of export records and 34 years of farm-gate prices show how quickly world prices reach farmers, how large a share they keep, and when coffee money arrives in the year.

Key takeaways

  1. Exports have more than doubled since 2015/16, to a record 8.4 million bags in 2025/26, worth about US$2.2 billion in each of the last two years. High
  2. Uganda's robusta sells close to the world price; its arabica at a discount of 15–35%, largely because much of it is sold unwashed. High
  3. Farmers keep a larger share than they used to: about 45% of the world robusta price in 1992–93, 65–72% through 2000–2015, 72–83% since 2022. High
  4. World prices reach farmers fast. Two-thirds or more of a change arrives within the month for robusta; arabica follows more slowly. High
  5. The 2024–25 boom was the world price, and it is unwinding. Farm-gate prices peaked in February 2025 and are down about a quarter since. High
  6. The robusta belt is warming fast, and heat in January–June cuts robusta exports, by about 9–20% per standard deviation depending on the check. A wet second half of the year lifts the next crop. Arabica shows no consistent signal. Medium
  7. Coffee money arrives June–September for robusta and February–May for arabica. Prices are calmer than maize month to month, but can halve within a year when the world cycle turns. High

Why the arabica gapMuch of Uganda's arabica is sold as unwashed drugar, which trades below washed coffee. Part of the 15–35% discount is therefore a processing gap: more washing stations, especially around Mt Elgon and the Rwenzoris, could narrow it.
03 · Farmers' share

Farmers now keep about three-quarters of the world price

Monthly (thin) and yearly average (dots)
ReadingThe share rose fast after Uganda liberalised coffee marketing in the early 1990s, and dips when world prices crash (52% in 2001), because traders' and exporters' costs don't fall with the price. Kiboko (dried cherry) sells for about half the FAQ price throughout, matching the weight lost in hulling, so farmers selling kiboko are paid about the same per kg of coffee.

Rises and fallsIn 2020–2026, world price rises reached robusta farmers almost in full within the month, while falls were passed on by about half (p = 0.06 for FAQ, 0.30 for kiboko; no difference in 1992–2015). That's the opposite of the common worry that traders pass falls on faster, and fits strong competition among 80-plus exporters during the boom. With one boom in the data, treat it as suggestive.

07 · Climate

The robusta belt is heating up, and heat costs exports

Daytime highs in Uganda's six coffee zones compared with their 1991–2020 average (ERA5-Land reanalysis). The robusta zones of Central, Greater Masaka, Busoga and the South-west are warming fastest; the arabica zones on Mt Elgon and the Rwenzoris more slowly. Rainfall has not fallen anywhere.

PriceTypical monthly moveTypical 12-month moveWorst 12-month fall
ReadingCoffee prices move about half as much as maize from one month to the next, so a season's income is fairly predictable once it starts. But coffee's worst falls are deeper than maize's: robusta lost more than half its real value in the 2001 world price crash. Income tied to coffee can halve within a year when the world cycle turns.
09 · What it means

Implications

These follow from the price and export evidence; the climate and deforestation analyses that come next will add to them.

Farmers

Watch the world price

  • Farm-gate prices are the world price with a short delay. When world prices move, Ugandan prices follow within weeks.
  • Kiboko and FAQ sellers are paid about the same per kg of coffee; hulling doesn't capture extra margin.
  • Washing arabica rather than selling it as drugar is where quality premiums lie.
  • In the robusta belt, shade trees, mulching and heat-tolerant varieties address the trend that is actually under way: heat.
Lenders and SACCOs

Time repayments to the harvest

  • Schedule coffee-linked repayments for June–September (robusta areas) and February–May (arabica areas).
  • Stress-test loans against a 50% fall in coffee prices over a year: robusta fell that far in 2001 and arabica in 2012.
  • The 2024–25 boom is already partly reversed; collateral and income valued at peak prices are overstated.
Policy

Quality, not price controls

  • The market already passes prices through fast and gives farmers a rising share.
  • The biggest gap is arabica processing: its discount to the world benchmark is 15–35%.
  • Publishing farm-gate prices consistently would close the 2015–2019 hole in the public record.

10 · Methods and limits

How this was done

Method

  • Exports and farm-gate prices read automatically from 80 monthly reports, each summary checked against the report's own tables
  • Farm-gate prices 1992–2015 from the Coffee Department's price table
  • Pass-through from an error-correction model of monthly price changes, with robust standard errors and separate terms for rises and falls
  • Exchange rate from the Coffee Department table to 2015, then the rate implied by WFP's price conversions
  • Coffee-zone climate: ERA5-Land temperature and CHIRPS rainfall over coffee districts, filtered by elevation for each coffee type

Limits

  • Farm-gate prices for late 2015–2019 are not published
  • Monthly export figures are preliminary (within about 2% of final figures)
  • Farm-gate prices are national averages; regional and buyer differences are not visible
  • Export values are nominal US dollars, which overstates long-run growth
